Here are a few other people who could have made De Morgan’s claim (listed by year of birth).

1892: [J.R.R. Tolkein](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolkein) was 44 in 1936

1806: [John Stuart Mill](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stuart_Mill) was 43 in 1849

1722: [Samuel Adams](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_adams) was 42 in 1764

1640: [Bernard Lamy](http://www.gap-system.org/~history/Biographies/Lamy.html), the mathematician, was 41 in 1681

1560: [Annibale Carracci](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annibale_Carracci), Italian painter was 40 in 1600

1482: [Maria of Aragon and Castile](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_of_Aragon_(1482-1517)), queen of Portugal would have been 39 in the year 1521 (she died in 1517)

1122: [Eleanor of Aquitaine](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_of_Aquitaine) was 34 in the year 1156

12: [Caligula](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caligula) was 4 in the year 16.

This sequence of birth years: 0, 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, 42, 56, 72, 90, 110… has an entry in [Sloane’s on-line encyclopedia of integer sequences](http://www.research.att.com/~njas/sequences/Seis.html). It is sequence number [A002378](http://www.research.att.com/~njas/sequences/A002378) and it has the name “Oblong (or promic, pronic, or heteromecic) numbers: n(n+1).” But this instance of the sequence is not listed in the comment section. When my kids were 5 and 10, I commented to my wife that she was the only one that year whose age wasn’t a multiple of 5. Her response was that it was OK, because the previous year I had been the only one in the family whose age wasn’t a perfect square.

Yes, we are physics geeks. And I wasn’t 40 when my first child was born.
