It is, in fact, a masterclass in behavioral psychology and UI/UX design.


Within the realm of game design analysis, there is the consideration of the way in which the system interacts with the desires of the human. Digital slots, for example, are not the mechanical wheel they once were. They have become sophisticated programs that are specifically designed to elicit certain neurological reactions. **Understanding** **the Brain’s Misinterpretation of Close Enough**

At its heart, the near-miss effect is a cognitive distortion. In a task that requires real skill, like archery or basketball, hitting the rim of the hoop can be a near miss, which can give you valuable feedback. It lets you know that your shot was almost perfect, and with a minuscule adjustment, you’ll score next time. Our brains are wired to see these events as encouraging because they signal progress.

The problem lies in the application of this logic when playing a purely random chance game. With a digital slot machine, each pull is a random event and is regulated by a Random Number Generator or RNG. Being one symbol away from a jackpot does not mean that you are close to winning the jackpot; it simply means you are one of millions of combinations that will lose. However, according to a study published by the [University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/reasoning-decision-making-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/DixHarJarFugShe_2011.pdf), near-miss experiences stimulate the same brain reward areas as actual winning experiences, which are the ventral striatum and the insula. This is like the brain cashing in on the thrill of the win before the reel even stops.

**How UI/UX Engineers Design the Almost-Win**

If you look at the interface of a modern slot machine game, you’ll see that the symbols on the screen are not just static images. They are part of a dynamic visual language. UX designers use a number of specific techniques to create a sense that a near miss is more impactful than a full miss, where the symbols are nowhere near a payline.

Here’s how they design the almost-win:

**Reel Deceleration and The Long Spin:**Do you see how the first two reels stop relatively quickly, but the third or the fifth appears to take forever to stop? That’s the anticipation phase of UX design. The game slows down the final reel so that the player’s brain has time to imagine the win.**Visual Overshooting:**In many cases, digital slots have their jackpot symbol located just above or below the line. This is important. If it were located three spaces away, it would be boring when it does not appear. By placing it just on the edge, it is more stimulating.**Auditory Cues:**Outcome perception is significantly influenced by sound, and a complete loss is typically accompanied by silence or a low-frequency thud. In the case of a near miss, the sound could resemble a swelling orchestral sound or a ding similar to the sound of a win, which could create the illusion that something positive has happened.

**The Feedback Loop: Why We Keep Spinning**

The threat of the near miss is that it does away with the stopping rule. Most times, when we are not successful at something, there is a level of frustration that comes with it, and this leads to us quitting the activity. However, the case with the near miss is that it creates a level of frustration, but the strength of the reaction is such that the individual feels they have won something or that the machine is ready to pay out.

And this is where the ethics of the game come into play. Even as the game is made interesting, it could end up being played for longer than the individual had planned. There are measures in place to counter this, such as the [reality check](https://www.gamblingcommission.gov.uk/manual/national-strategy-to-reduce-gambling-harms/rts-13-time-requirements-and-reality-checks), which helps to ensure that the individual’s psychology of the almost-win does not interfere with their financial boundaries.

**Structural Differences: Virtual Reels vs. Physical Reality**

In the old days of slots, a near miss was purely accidental based on the physical stops on a reel. In the digital world, software engineers use a method called clustering or virtual reel mapping. This method allows the software to create a single losing result and map it to multiple positions on the screen.

For instance, the jackpot symbol might have a 1 in 1,000 probability of being chosen by the RNG. The surrounding symbols are mapped to appear much more frequently. This creates a screen that appears to constantly tease the user, even though the probability of a win remains the same. It’s a very intelligent mapping that puts the user in a state of high arousal, even during a losing run.

**Wrapping Up the Spin**

Knowing the psychology behind near misses doesn’t have to spoil the enjoyment of the game. On the contrary, it makes you a wiser player. By realizing that a near miss is simply another form of a losing experience, you can enjoy the display without succumbing to the fallacy that you are entitled to a winning experience.

The beauty of modern digital slots is the ability to elicit such emotions using nothing more than pixels and code. It’s a testament to the extent to which our minds are attuned to the thrill of the chase.
